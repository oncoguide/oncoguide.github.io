**If your cancer has spread to your bones, you may already know denosumab by its steady monthly rhythm — or by its brand name, Xgeva.** It's a small injection that quietly protects your skeleton, and for many people it becomes part of life for a long time.

That's exactly why the details are worth understanding: what it actually does, the **blood tests you need before every injection**, the welcome shift from a **monthly** injection to one **every three months**, the step-down ("de-escalation") ladder your doctors may follow — and the safety rules that protect you, above all **why you must never simply stop it on your own.** None of this replaces your oncology team, who decide your dosing; it's here so you can follow what's happening and ask sharper questions.

---

## What denosumab is

Denosumab is a **monoclonal antibody** — a lab-made protein — that blocks a body signal called **RANKL**. RANKL switches on **osteoclasts**, the cells that dissolve bone. When cancer spreads to bone, it hijacks this signal to eat away at the skeleton. By switching RANKL off, denosumab calms that bone destruction. It is given as an injection **under the skin (120 mg)**, and it works the same way **no matter which cancer** has spread to the bone.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}
The same drug exists in a **lower dose (Prolia, 60 mg)** for osteoporosis. This guide is about the **cancer dose — Xgeva, 120 mg** — which is stronger and given more often.
{{< /callout >}}

## When it's used

In cancer, denosumab is used when there are **bone metastases** from a solid tumour, to prevent **skeletal-related events** — the serious bone complications:

- fractures from little or no injury,
- compression of the spinal cord,
- needing radiotherapy or surgery to a bone,
- dangerously high blood calcium (hypercalcaemia).

The main alternative is a **bisphosphonate** such as **zoledronic acid (Zometa)**. Both protect bone well. They differ in a few trade-offs: denosumab is more potent and needs no kidney dose-adjustment, but its effect is **reversible** (which matters hugely if it is ever stopped — see safety); zoledronic acid does **not** cause a rebound when stopped but is limited in poor kidney function.

## The blood tests before every injection

Denosumab pulls calcium **into** bone, so it can push your blood calcium **down** — sometimes dangerously. That is why you should be **topped up on calcium and vitamin D** and have a few values checked **before each dose**:

| Blood test | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Calcium** (blood level) | Denosumab can cause low calcium; it must be in range *before* each injection |
| **Vitamin D** (25-OH-D) | Low vitamin D makes low calcium worse; it should be sufficient (≈ ≥30 ng/mL) |
| **Magnesium** | If magnesium is low, calcium is very hard to correct |
| **PTH** (parathyroid hormone) | Helps your team understand how your body is handling calcium |
| **Kidney function** (creatinine / eGFR) | Severe low calcium is more likely when kidneys work poorly. *Note:* some cancer medicines make the usual creatinine estimate read falsely low — ask whether a **cystatin C**–based estimate is more accurate for you. |

{{< callout type="important" >}}
Take your **calcium + vitamin D** every day throughout treatment unless your doctor says otherwise — this is not optional, it is what keeps the injection safe. After your first doses, calcium is often re-checked around **day 7–14**. Report **tingling, numbness, muscle cramps or spasms** the same day. (Lab reports show calcium in a few ways — ask your team which value they track for you: total, ionised, or "corrected".)
{{< /callout >}}

## From every 4 weeks to every 12 weeks

For years, denosumab was given **every 4 weeks**. We now have strong evidence that, after an initial **loading phase of about 4 monthly doses**, switching to **every 12 weeks** protects bone **just as well** — with **fewer side effects and far fewer injections.**

The pivotal trial — **REDUSE (SAKK 96/12)**, a phase 3 randomised trial in about 1,380 patients with bone metastases from breast or prostate cancer — found every-12-weeks **non-inferior** to every-4-weeks for symptomatic skeletal events (hazard ratio **1.04**, 90% CI 0.91–1.20), while side effects dropped ([REDUSE, *Journal of Clinical Oncology* 2026](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2026.44.16_suppl.1004)):

| | Every 4 weeks | Every 12 weeks |
|---|---|---|
| **Bone protection** | reference | **just as good** (HR 1.04) |
| **Low calcium** (any grade) | ~46% | **~30%** |
| **Jaw osteonecrosis (ONJ)** | ~8.5% | **~6.9%** |
| **Injections per year** | ~13 | **~4** |

**What this means for you:** the **same injection, same dose** — just **less often**. Fewer clinic visits, a **lower risk of low calcium and jaw problems**, and far fewer injections over the years (which matters, because some risks add up dose by dose). The switch is **not automatic everywhere** — after your loading doses, ask your oncologist whether you qualify to move to every 12 weeks.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}
REDUSE studied breast and prostate cancer. Because denosumab works the **same way** in every cancer, many oncologists apply the every-12-weeks approach to other solid tumours (such as lung) too — though formal guidelines are still catching up. Ask your oncologist what fits your situation.
{{< /callout >}}

## The Cheung de-escalation ladder

Beyond the move to every-12-weeks, some patients whose bone disease is **well-controlled** may step down further over time. This idea is **informed by the framework** of Cheung and colleagues ([*The Oncologist*, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9732234/)), who set out how denosumab might be de-escalated or stopped in cancer. One of their core principles: once you have had more than a couple of doses, doctors **try hard to avoid simply stopping** — because of the rebound risk described below. The rungs below combine that thinking with the REDUSE schedule.

Think of it as rungs you step **down** only while your disease stays controlled — **always under your oncologist's direction:**

<div class="deesc-ladder">

1. **Loading — every 4 weeks (about 4 doses).** Monthly injections to fully "switch off" bone breakdown.
2. **Maintenance — every 12 weeks.** The main, evidence-based step-down (REDUSE). Same dose, given every 3 months.
3. **Extension — about every 16 weeks.** In selected, very stable patients the gap may be stretched a little further, with closer monitoring. This is at the **edge of the evidence** — no completed trial has confirmed it.
4. **Conditional stop — with a bisphosphonate "bridge."** Only if strict criteria are met (long, durable control on scans and bone markers) may denosumab be stopped — but **never alone**: a dose of zoledronic acid is given about **6 months later** (on your doctor's schedule) to prevent rebound (see safety).
5. **Surveillance only.** Off denosumab, with continued scans and blood tests; treatment **restarts immediately** if any sign of bone activity returns.

</div>

Rungs 3–5 involve careful criteria and judgement — they are **clinician decisions**, made for each person individually. The ladder can also step back **up** at any time if the disease reactivates.

## Safety — the rules that matter

{{< callout type="warning" >}}
**Stopping denosumab is your team's decision — never a do-it-yourself one. And pausing a dose for a safety reason (such as low calcium) is *not* the same as stopping.** Here is why it matters: unlike bisphosphonates, denosumab's effect is **reversible**. If it is stopped *without a plan*, bone breakdown can **rebound 6–9 months later** and lead to **spontaneous spine fractures** — sometimes in people who felt perfectly well. So denosumab is **never stopped on its own**: if stopping is ever right for you, your team **bridges with a bisphosphonate (zoledronic acid)**, given about **6 months after the last dose** on a schedule they set ([ESMO guidance](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2020.07.019); [bridging protocol](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgaa756)), and they usually consider stopping only after about **2 years** of durable control. This rebound risk is well documented, including in cancer patients ([case report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9109183/)).
{{< /callout >}}

**Jaw (osteonecrosis of the jaw, ONJ) — see a dentist first.** Have a **dental check-up and any needed dental work done *before* starting**, keep up good oral hygiene, and tell every dentist that you are on denosumab. Plan non-urgent extractions carefully. The risk rises with the **number of doses** — another reason the every-12-weeks schedule helps.

**Low calcium.** Keep taking your **calcium + vitamin D**, and report tingling or cramps (see the blood-test section above).

**Kidneys.** Kidney function is checked before dosing; very poor kidney function raises the low-calcium risk.

### Vascular safety — calcium and vitamin D over the long haul

Because denosumab makes daily **calcium and vitamin D essential**, people who take it for years reasonably ask: *could all that calcium harm my heart or arteries?*

The evidence-based, reassuring answer: in generally healthy adults, calcium from food and supplements — **kept within the recommended daily upper limit (about 2,000–2,500 mg/day in total)** — has **no proven link to heart or blood-vessel disease** ([National Osteoporosis Foundation & American Society for Preventive Cardiology guideline, *Annals of Internal Medicine* 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27776362/)). The practical takeaways:

- Get calcium from **food first**, and use **supplements to *reach* — not exceed — your target.**
- **Don't mega-dose.** More is not better; stay under the upper limit.
- Keep **vitamin D sufficient** — it helps your body use calcium properly.
- Have your calcium target set **with your team**, especially if you have kidney or heart conditions.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}
Some clinicians also discuss **vitamin K2**, on the idea that it helps direct calcium into bone rather than arteries. The evidence for this is **not established** — don't add it on your own; raise it with your team if you're interested.
{{< /callout >}}

## Questions worth asking your oncologist

<div class="onco-faq">

<details>
<summary>After my loading doses, can I move to every 12 weeks?</summary>

Often, yes. After about four monthly "loading" doses, the REDUSE trial showed every-12-weeks protects bone **just as well** as every-4-weeks, with fewer side effects. It is not automatic everywhere, so ask your oncologist whether you qualify once your loading doses are done.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Are my calcium and vitamin D checked before each injection — and am I on the right supplements?</summary>

They should be. Because denosumab can lower blood calcium, your team should confirm your **calcium** (and ideally vitamin D, magnesium and kidney function) are in range **before each dose**, and you should take **daily calcium + vitamin D** throughout treatment. If you are unsure, ask what your last levels were and exactly which supplements and doses you should be taking.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Have I had a dental check before starting — and is dental work planned safely?</summary>

A **dental check-up and any needed dental work should ideally be done before your first dose**, because denosumab slightly raises the risk of osteonecrosis of the jaw. Keep good oral hygiene, tell every dentist that you are on denosumab, and plan any non-urgent extractions together with your oncology and dental teams.

</details>

<details>
<summary>If we ever consider stopping, what is the plan to protect my spine (the bisphosphonate "bridge")?</summary>

Denosumab should **never be stopped abruptly** — stopping without a plan can trigger **rebound spine fractures** about 6–9 months later. If stopping is ever appropriate, your team **bridges with a bisphosphonate (zoledronic acid)**, usually around **6 months after the last dose**. Ask what the specific plan and timing would be for you.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Am I taking the right amount of calcium — enough for safety, but within the recommended limit?</summary>

The aim is **enough** calcium and vitamin D to keep the injection safe, **without exceeding** the recommended daily upper limit (about **2,000–2,500 mg/day** in total, from food plus supplements) — an amount considered safe for your heart and blood vessels. Favour **food sources**, use supplements to *reach* (not exceed) your target, and have your team set the right amount for you.

</details>

</div>
